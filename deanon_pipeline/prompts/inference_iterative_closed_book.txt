=== HOW TO ANSWER ===
Your answer must be DERIVED from the evidence, and you show that by working through
the derivation below before you name anyone. The identifier is the OUTPUT of steps
A-D, never an input you justify afterwards.

Why the order matters: it is easy to recognise a person from a few attributes and
then find some line mentioning them — the evidence usually does contain the
identifier somewhere. That proves nothing: it shows the identifier EXISTS in the
graph, not that the graph POINTS to it. Working A -> D forward is what separates
the two.

Recognising someone is not a failure and not forbidden — step E asks you to report it
honestly. What is forbidden is letting recognition stand in for derivation.

This applies to [PERSON_A], [PERSON_B], ... too: they are other anonymized people. You
cannot resolve them from your own knowledge, so treat them as opaque nodes — never
output one as the target, and do not spend requests on them.

WHAT COUNTS AS AN IDENTIFIER
Every person in this graph — apart from [TARGET PERSON] and the bracketed [PERSON_A] /
[PERSON_B] anonymized nodes — is written as a stable code such as IND_0421. That code IS
the identifier: it is a complete entity name, not a hidden real name waiting to be
decoded. There is no hidden "real identity" behind it to recover — resolving
[TARGET PERSON] means determining which existing graph entity is co-referential with
it, and that entity's name is whatever atomic identifier the graph already uses for
it (a readable name such as "Michael J. Fox", or a graph-native code such as
"IND_0421" — both are complete entity names, not one being a stand-in for the other).
Your answer is one of these identifiers.

Do not infer, guess, recover, decode, expand, or silently resolve any IND_#### code to a
different person's identity using internal or external knowledge — neither to reach an
answer nor to reason your way to one. Distinguish IND_0421 from IND_0932 the same way you
would distinguish two candidates by name: by which facts are attached to each one in the
evidence, and only those.

Only the bracketed [PERSON_A] / [PERSON_B] labels and /m/... strings are off-limits, and
only because those mark the anonymized nodes themselves.

So: seeing nothing but IND_#### codes in the evidence is NOT a reason to answer UNKNOWN.
Intersect the anchors and answer with whichever identifier the evidence uses — that
identifier is your final answer, not a placeholder standing in for another one.

=== EVIDENCE FORMAT ===
Each evidence line is of the form ['head', 'relation', 'tail'] and means
"head's relation is tail." The relation is the FULL path, and its prefix tells you
the TYPE of fact:
  - /people/person/...      → a fact about a PERSON
  - /location/...           → a fact about a PLACE. A line such as
    ['Idaho', '/location/statistical_region/religions./location/religion_percentage/religion',
     'Pentecostalism'] is a population statistic about the STATE — it says nothing
    about any particular resident, and can never single one out.
Always check the relation prefix before using a line to link two entities.

=== THE EVIDENCE IS NOISY — SEARCH IT, DON'T JUST READ IT ===
The evidence is a multi-hop subgraph pulled around [TARGET PERSON]. Most of it is
IRRELEVANT: facts about other entities that merely sit near the target in the
graph. Expect the useful signal to be a small minority of lines.

Because of that, do NOT conclude "nothing identifies the target" after one pass:
- Read past the generic attributes. Scan specifically for lines carrying a specific
  entity (a person, film, award, school, company, or organisation) that is connected
  to [TARGET PERSON] by a chain of facts you can follow line by line.
- The target's co-referential entity may already be sitting somewhere in the
  evidence, several hops away, reached through an intermediate node. Finding it
  there and citing the chain is exactly what you are supposed to do.
- Try MULTIPLE hypotheses before settling. For each candidate entity in the evidence,
  ask: "could the chain of facts make THIS the target?" Discard the ones that
  break on a cited fact, and say which fact broke them.
- Distinguish two very different situations, and never confuse them:
    (a) the evidence has no chain to any entity  -> UNKNOWN is correct;
    (b) a chain exists but you stopped looking    -> that is a failure, keep searching.

Rules:
- Work with entities the evidence identifies (a person's atomic identifier — readable
  name or code — a film, award, school, company) tied to [TARGET PERSON] by a chain of
  facts you can follow line by line.
- Complete DERIVATION steps A-E before PREDICTIONS. An identifier that does not appear
  in your own step B list cannot be used in PREDICTIONS — the answer comes out of the
  derivation.
- EVERY non-UNKNOWN prediction MUST end with "GROUNDING: facts <numbers>" citing the exact
  evidence line numbers your answer rests on. At least one of them must be a line that
  CONTAINS THE PREDICTED IDENTIFIER — if no such line exists, you do not have an answer:
  UNKNOWN.
- Cite only the DECISIVE lines — typically 2 to 6 of them. UNKNOWN answers need no GROUNDING.

=== HOW TO PULL MORE EVIDENCE (this is the core of your job) ===
You are NOT limited to the facts already shown. Each round you may request more
evidence from the graph, and a later round will show you what came back. Being
UNKNOWN now is fine — but if more graph facts could plausibly break the tie, you
should ASK for them rather than stopping.

You have two request types. Prefer the precise one:

FIRST, THE ONE THING THAT DECIDES WHETHER A REQUEST PAYS OFF
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
[TARGET PERSON] is anonymized: the facts directly attached to its anonymized node
are typically just a handful of lines — the sensitive context you were given (a
religion, an ethnicity, a currency) plus occasionally one or two other direct facts
like an award or a film credit, if the evidence happened to include them. It
usually has no profession, education, or spouse fact attached to that node. Most of
what would identify TARGET belongs to candidate entities elsewhere in the graph —
entities you have not yet resolved as co-referential with TARGET. Your task is to
determine which existing graph entity is co-referential with [TARGET PERSON]; there
is no separate hidden identity to uncover beyond that.

So asking for a relation OF TARGET usually returns little or nothing. Measured over
210 targets:

    NEED_RELATION: <anything> of TARGET                →  0.05 facts per request
    NEED_RELATION: <anything> of <a visible entity>    →  5.18 facts per request

A hundred-fold difference. Anchor every request on a visible, non-anonymized entity
already present in the evidence, and ask for the relation that would make it LIST
PEOPLE. This is usually the most targeted way to bring candidate entities into view
(EXPLORE on a hub entity such as a university or company can also surface several
at once — see request type 2 below).

1. NEED_RELATION: <relation> of <node>     ← PRECISE, strongly preferred
   Pulls ONLY that one relation of that one node.
   GOOD — anchored on a visible entity, asking for its people:
     NEED_RELATION: student of Michigan State University
     NEED_RELATION: winner of Academy Award for Best Sound
     NEED_RELATION: actor of Austin Powers in Goldmember
     NEED_RELATION: people of poliomyelitis
   WASTED — anchored on TARGET (returns nothing, costs you the round):
     NEED_RELATION: award of TARGET
     NEED_RELATION: education of TARGET
     NEED_RELATION: profession of TARGET
   The <relation> is matched as a substring against the node's relation paths,
   so short keywords work well. The ones that actually return people:
   student, actor, artist, winner, nominee, people, participant, member,
   crewmember, politician, founder.

2. EXPLORE: <visible entity>     (alias: IDENTIFY: <visible entity>)
   Pulls everything about that node. Use when you have no specific hypothesis
   yet and need a broad look at an entity — this can also expose several
   candidate entities at once (e.g. a university's students, faculty, and
   founders together).

TWO CONSTRAINTS, both enforced — a violating request is silently dropped and
you waste the round:
  (a) <node> must ALREADY BE VISIBLE in the evidence above (TARGET, or an entity
      appearing in a fact). You may not request a node you merely suspect
      exists from outside knowledge.
  (b) <node> must NOT be a [PERSON_X] placeholder or a /m/... identifier. Those
      are anonymized and cannot be resolved from a request; asking about them
      is a dead end.

THE STRATEGY, IN THE ORDER YOU SHOULD APPLY IT

Step 1 — List the entities [TARGET PERSON] touches directly.
  These are your anchors. An anchor is only an entity directly connected to
  [TARGET PERSON] by one evidence edge, in either direction — including a node
  reached through TARGET's own sensitive facts (the disease, the party, the
  ethnicity). Do not promote a node reached through an EXTRA hop (e.g. the
  disease's risk factors, the religion's founding figures) into an anchor
  unless that node is itself also directly linked to [TARGET PERSON].

Step 2 — Rank them by expected selectivity, using only what the evidence shows.
  A 30-member film cast or a 5-person award edition beats a nation of millions.
  If the evidence shows a node's degree, use it; otherwise prefer structurally
  specific anchors (a particular film, a specific award edition, a school) over
  broad demographic categories (a nationality, a mass religion) — but do not
  estimate an anchor's real-world population from outside knowledge. Ask the
  more specific ones first — a request that returns 200 names has told you
  little.

Step 3 — Ask that node to list its people.
  "student of <university>", "actor of <film>", "winner of <award>",
  "people of <disease>". Now candidate entities enter the evidence.

Step 4 — Intersect, don't accept.
  A single shared anchor is usually not enough on its own, especially when
  that anchor is broad (a film cast holds 7 people, an award ceremony ~30) —
  prefer the candidate supported by the most independent TARGET anchors. But
  do not apply a fixed "must have two anchors" rule: one anchor is sufficient
  when the evidence itself shows it already isolates a single candidate (see
  Example D). When candidates trade off on the two criteria — one shares MORE
  anchors, another shares a MORE SPECIFIC one — specificity wins: a candidate
  uniquely isolated by one highly specific anchor (an award edition with one
  winner) outranks a candidate merely sharing two broad anchors (nationality
  and gender) that many other people would also satisfy. Anchor count is a
  useful default only when specificity is roughly comparable across anchors.

Step 5 — Break ties with the sensitive facts you were given.
  You know TARGET's religion / ethnicity / cause of death. Do not use these to
  recall or guess an identity from prior knowledge — use them only to filter
  candidates already surfaced by steps 1-4, and only against facts actually
  retrieved (e.g. a candidate whose retrieved cause-of-death fact contradicts
  TARGET's is eliminated by that fact — not by outside knowledge of whether a
  real person is alive or dead).

If a specific relation request returns nothing, do not repeat that same
node-relation request unchanged. You may still ask a different relation of the
same node (a university that returned nothing for "actor" may still answer
"student" or "founder") if it could plausibly expose candidate entities.
